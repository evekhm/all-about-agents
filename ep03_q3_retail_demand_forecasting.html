Production Retail Demand Forecasting and Honest LLM Scoping

A retail stakeholder asks if a language model can forecast store-level SKU demand; we cover how retail forecasting works from the ground up and place each model family where it excels.

In this episode, we tackle a classic scoping question: whether a language model can run store-level SKU demand forecasting. We break down the four retail demand mechanics (stockout uncensoring, three SKU regimes, quantile safety stock, and hierarchical reconciliation), explain why LightGBM, TimesFM (BigQuery AI.FORECAST), and count models own the numerical core, and place language models in three backtest-gated sidecars.

Retail demand forecasting predicts future unit demand across a massive three-dimensional panel (Store × SKU × Day or Week) spanning tens of thousands of products across hundreds or thousands of locations. Every downstream physical decision depends on those numbers: automated vendor purchase orders, distribution-center pallet allocation, store labor scheduling, and perishable markdowns. Four domain mechanics explain why specialized tabular and time-series models own the numerical core.

1. The Four Mechanics That Define Retail Demand

  • Stockout Censoring (Observed Sales vs. True Demand): Point-of-sale (POS) systems record units sold, which equals true customer demand only while inventory sits on the shelf. If a store stocks 12 cartons of strawberries on Saturday morning, sells out by 11:00 a.m., and turns away 25 shoppers in the afternoon, POS logs record sales = 12 on Saturday and sales = 0 on Sunday, since the next delivery arrives on Monday. Feeding raw POS zeros directly into any forecasting model creates a replenishment death spiral: the model mistakes out-of-stock zeros for zero shopper interest, lowers next week's forecast, orders even fewer units, and triggers earlier stockouts. Production pipelines always join daily inventory_on_hand snapshots first, mask stockout periods (inventory == 0) as right-censored observations, and impute unconstrained latent demand before model fitting.
  • Three Distinct SKU Regimes (Dense, Cold-Start, and Intermittent Tail): High-velocity staples (milk, bananas, cola) generate smooth daily curves driven by price elasticity, promotions, holidays, payday cycles, and sibling-SKU cannibalization (putting 12-packs on 30% promo depresses sales of the 6-packs next to them). Newly launched seasonal apparel or electronics arrive with zero historical sales (cold start). Meanwhile, 50% to 70% of a typical catalog sits in the slow-moving intermittent tail (auto parts, specialty cookware, fringe sizes), where most days record legitimate zero sales punctuated by occasional 1-to-3-unit purchases.
  • Asymmetric Replenishment Economics and Quantile Forecasts (P10 / P50 / P90): Ordering strictly to a median point forecast (P50) leaves stores out of stock on roughly half of supplier lead-time cycles. Retail replenishment solves an asymmetric Newsvendor trade-off using probabilistic quantiles trained with pinball loss: fresh cut fruit with high 48-hour spoilage cost is ordered near P55–P60, whereas high-margin shelf-stable batteries with high lost-sale cost and negligible holding cost are ordered at P85–P95. One critical mathematical rule governs lead times: daily P90 predictions are per-day marginals, so summing seven daily P90 values assumes perfect error correlation across all seven days and inflates safety stock; production pipelines forecast directly over the supplier lead-time window (such as 7-day cumulative demand) or simulate joint trajectories.
  • Hierarchical Coherence and Retail Error Metrics: Merchandising VPs plan national category budgets, regional warehouses allocate pallets, and local stores replenish shelves. Independent models rarely add up across levels, so production systems apply Minimum Trace (MinT) / weighted least-squares hierarchical reconciliation so noisy store-SKU forecasts borrow statistical strength from category trends and sum cleanly to regional totals. Teams evaluate accuracy using WAPE (Weighted Absolute Percentage Error: sum(|actual - forecast|) / sum(actual), avoiding division-by-zero blowups on zero-sale days), Forecast Bias (sum(forecast - actual) / sum(actual) to catch systematic over- or under-ordering), and Pinball Loss across target quantiles.

2. Why Text LLMs Fail on Raw Time Series and Which Numerical Models Own the Core

Passing historical sales arrays into a conversational text LLM produces miscalibrated numbers, breaks seasonality, and explodes inference budgets across millions of SKU-store combinations.

Autoregressive language models split numbers into arbitrary subword text tokens, lack continuous inductive bias for periodic frequencies or lag autocorrelation, shift seasonal phase if a single date is missing, and cannot calibrate pinball-loss quantiles. Controlled ablations confirm that replacing text-LLM backbones with lightweight attention or linear patching layers matches or improves forecasting accuracy at orders-of-magnitude lower compute cost (Tan et al., NeurIPS 2024). Production retail architectures instead run a three-part numerical ensemble (Figure 1):

  1. Global Tabular GBDTs (LightGBM / XGBoost) or Dense Time-Series Encoders (TiDE): Own mature, promotion-sensitive SKUs because tree splits and dense covariates capture non-linear price thresholds, promo mechanics (BOGO vs. end-cap display), cross-SKU cannibalization, calendar flags, and local weather.
  2. Pretrained Time-Series Foundation Models (TimesFM, Das et al., ICML 2024): TimesFM is a patch-decoder foundation model pretrained directly on about 100 billion real and synthetic numerical time-series points, which sets it apart from a text LLM. Exposed directly in SQL via BigQuery AI.FORECAST (Google Cloud, 2026), it produces strong zero-shot trend, multi-seasonality, and prediction-interval baselines for cold-start stores, short-history SKUs, and rapid SQL analytics without per-SKU training pipelines.
  3. Discrete Count and Hurdle Models (Croston-TSB, Negative Binomial, Two-Stage Hurdle GBDT): Own intermittent slow-moving tail SKUs where continuous instance normalization degenerates on near-zero variance, modeling demand occurrence probability (P(demand > 0)) separately from conditional order size.

The SQL sketch below illustrates running zero-shot probabilistic 14-day forecasting across store-SKU series in BigQuery using AI.FORECAST (backed by TimesFM) after joining inventory snapshots to replace stockout zeros with imputed latent demand:

Zero-Shot Probabilistic SKU-Store Baseline Forecast over Stockout-Uncensored Demand (BigQuery AI.FORECAST)
WITH uncensored_daily_panel AS (
  SELECT
    store_id,
    sku_id,
    sales_date,
    -- Use observed POS units when stock is available; use imputed latent demand on stockout days
    CASE
      WHEN ending_inventory_units > 0 THEN CAST(pos_units_sold AS FLOAT64)
      ELSE imputed_latent_demand_units
    END AS true_demand_units
  FROM `retail_prod.daily_store_sku_panel`
  WHERE sales_date >= DATE_SUB(CURRENT_DATE(), INTERVAL 365 DAY)
    AND sku_regime IN ('SEASONAL_SHORT_HISTORY', 'CORE_BASELINE')
)
SELECT
  store_id,
  sku_id,
  forecast_timestamp,
  forecast_value AS p50_demand,                     -- Median (50th percentile) forecast
  prediction_interval_lower_bound AS p10_demand,    -- Lower bound at confidence_level = 0.80
  prediction_interval_upper_bound AS p90_demand     -- Upper bound (90th percentile safety stock)
FROM
  AI.FORECAST(
    (SELECT * FROM uncensored_daily_panel),
    data_col => 'true_demand_units',
    timestamp_col => 'sales_date',
    id_cols => ['store_id', 'sku_id'],
    horizon => 14,
    confidence_level => 0.80
  );

3. Where Language and Multimodal Models Genuinely Fit and How to Avoid Overselling

Important real-world demand shocks live in unstructured supplier emails, PDF promotional flyers, and catalog photos that tabular models cannot read natively, while asking a language model to predict raw SKU numbers directly produces noisy, miscalibrated orders.

Place multimodal and language models in three bounded sidecars around the numerical core, and hold every extracted feature to a strict backtest gate:

  • Sidecar A · Upstream Exogenous Feature & Cold-Start Analog Extraction: Parse unstructured vendor delay notices, regional promo calendars, and multimodal product images + fabric descriptions into typed tabular columns (promo_mechanic = "BOGO", supplier_lead_time_delta_days = +5) or nearest-neighbor historical SKU analogs for Day-0 launches. Admit an LLM-extracted covariate into production only if historical walk-forward backtesting proves it reduces out-of-sample WAPE by a meaningful margin (for instance, >= 3%) and passes a timestamp-shuffle check against spurious correlation.
  • Sidecar B · Auditable Shock-Adjustment Proposals for Unprecedented Events: When a one-off event occurs outside historical training data (a temporary store remodel closure, port strike, or sudden tariff change), have the model draft a human-reviewed proposal card specifying bounded post-processing operators on the numerical quantile curve (such as OVERWRITE = 0 during closure dates or a bounded RESCALE multiplier) with linked source evidence.
  • Sidecar C · Merchandiser Explainability & What-If Copilot: Translate a planner's natural-language question ("Why is the Northeast cold-brew forecast up 28% next week, and what if we shift the 20% promo by seven days?") into parameterized calls against SHAP feature attributions and the GBDT/TimesFM forecasting service, and return grounded explanations and exception summaries.
