Episode 03 · AI System Design Interviews · Question 01

Making an LLM Trustworthy for an Enterprise Customer

How far we can see into a model's reasoning traces, what "black box" means to a customer beyond latent activations, and the system layers that make an LLM solution transparent.

My goal here is to answer one of the most common questions in enterprise AI system-design interviews: how to make a language model trustworthy to a skeptical customer. We start inside the model—what reasoning traces exist, what granularity each reaches, and why open weights still require multi-step instrumentation—before stepping outside to the nine stakeholder concerns, seven transparent system layers, and six architectural alternatives.

The 30-second answer: The model's weights and latent activations remain opaque: knowledge is spread across billions of weights, per-request activations are discarded after each token, and thought summaries, raw thinking tokens, and circuit tracing leave the causal reason behind a specific answer unverified. Trust therefore comes from the surrounding system, which makes inputs, sources, limits, evaluations, and history transparent across three pillars:
1. Verify every output: ground answers in cited sources, measure quality on a golden set, and let code make high-stakes decisions on verified facts.
2. Control and audit execution: enforce code guardrails, pin model versions, protect data governance boundaries, and log every step for replay.
3. Roll out safely: match human review to risk, and graduate from shadow mode to automation behind an instant kill switch.

What we will walk through step by step:

  1. Inside the model: why weights and activations are opaque for both APIs and open weights, and the three levels of reasoning-trace granularity.
  2. From "black box" to a checkable system: the nine distinct stakeholder concerns and six business risks that matter outside the weights, and the seven engineering controls (A–G) with the Google Cloud services that address each concern.
  3. Keep the decision out of the model: a prior-authorization worked example comparing a trace-based denial against a verifiable hybrid design, followed by the six architectural splits (Table 3).
  4. Summary and follow-up questions: spoken wrap-up and likely interview follow-ups.

Calling a language model a "black box" points to four general engineering requirements: behavioral predictability, output verifiability, enforceable boundaries, and defensibility under audit and regulatory scrutiny. Understanding how to meet those requirements begins with the model itself—specifically, which internal reasoning traces are observable, the granularity each trace exposes, and where the boundary of opaque weights and latent activations lies.

1. Tracing a Model's Reasoning: What You Can and Cannot See

You can read what a model writes while it reasons, but not reliably why it reached its answer. This section explains where reasoning actually happens, what each kind of trace shows, and what each one can prove.

Weights and activations. A model works with two kinds of numbers. Weights are the billions of values learned during training. They are fixed and identical for every request. The model's knowledge is spread across all of them at once, so there is no rule or line you can point to and read. Activations are the numbers the model computes from those weights and your input while it handles one request: its working state, which exists only during that request. In the formula y = 2x + 3, the 2 and the 3 play the role of weights; with x = 5, the intermediate 10 and the result 13 play the role of activations. This is the same for every LLM, whether it runs behind a proprietary API or on open weights. The difference is only who can see the numbers.

One token at a time. A model writes its answer one token, a word or part of a word, at a time. For each token it runs one forward pass: the input and everything written so far flow up through every layer, each layer computes new activations, and the last layer turns them into probabilities for the next token. One token is sampled, and that pass's activations are discarded. Each layer's keys and values are cached for speed, but they can be rebuilt from the tokens. The new token is appended to the input, and the next pass begins. Figure 1 shows four steps of this loop on the prior-authorization case from Section 3: after reading "ibuprofen", the model writes "needs", "6 wk", "PT" (physical therapy), "fail".

Figure 1 — One forward pass per token: activations are computed through every layer and discarded once the next token is sampled, and only the generated token is fed back as the next input. To save time, the model keeps some numbers from earlier tokens instead of recalculating them (the KV cache). Rerunning the same text would produce exactly the same numbers, so the cache is purely a shortcut: the text so far (the prompt plus everything the model has generated) is the only information carried from one step to the next. A separate pass condenses the raw trace into a thought summary.

What this means. Everything the model weighs while choosing a token lives in activations that are never written down. The only thing carried from one step to the next is the text so far: the prompt plus every token the model has generated. That generated text is the model's notepad; the thinking behind each token stays in the discarded activations.

Three things you can look at (the three levels in Figure 1):

  • Level 1 · Thought summaries. What you see: a short prose summary of the model's reasoning. Who can see it: anyone using a reasoning API such as the Gemini API (Gemini API thinking docs, 2026); this is usually all a proprietary API returns. What it proves: little about why the model answered as it did. A summary sits two steps removed from the computation: it condenses tokens that were themselves sampled from activations.
  • Level 2 · Raw thinking tokens. What you see: the actual tokens the model wrote to itself before answering. Who can see it: users of open-weight reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek Thinking Mode, which exposes the full reasoning trace in reasoning_content or <think> tags); proprietary APIs usually keep them with the provider. What it proves: what the model wrote; the cause of its answer stays out of view. On hard multi-step problems, the model has to write intermediate results down to use them in later steps, which makes the trace useful working memory to monitor (Korbak et al., Jul 2025). But it can leave out the real reason, as the hint test below shows.
  • Level 3 · Activations (circuit tracing). What you see: which internal features fired and how they pushed the model toward its next token. Who can see it: only teams running open weights on infrastructure they control, with specialist tooling. What it proves: part of the real internal path for one prompt. Attribution graphs (Lindsey et al., Mar 2025)—brought to open-weight models by the circuit-tracing library—and sparse autoencoders (Gemma Scope 2) capture only a fraction of the computation, at research cost.
Example: the hint test. Researchers ask a model a multiple-choice question with a hint added, such as "A Stanford professor indicates the answer is (A)," pointing to an answer the model would not otherwise pick. When the hint changes the answer, they check whether the model's reasoning mentions it. Often it does not: the trace builds a plausible argument for (A), like a student who saw the answer key and writes out a full solution without mentioning it. In these tests, reasoning models often disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases (Chen et al., May 2025), extending earlier results on prompted chain-of-thought (Turpin et al., NeurIPS 2023). Newer models follow planted hints less often, which makes this test harder to run, but it does not make the traces proof.

What it takes to look inside (Level 3). Downloading open weights does not explain anything by itself: the checkpoint is a static archive of trained numbers. Explaining one prompt takes three engineering steps:

  1. Record the activations. Host the model on infrastructure you control and attach hooks that save each layer's activations as the prompt runs.
  2. Split them into readable features. A single activation value usually mixes many unrelated concepts, so a second, specially trained model, a sparse autoencoder or transcoder, splits the activations into features a person can try to label. Each one has to be trained for that exact model.
  3. Trace how features influence each other. Link the active features across layers into an attribution graph showing which ones pushed the final token up or down. This is an expensive, research-grade investigation that still captures only part of the computation.

The way out: separate reading from deciding. Have the model extract structured facts (with exact source quotes) into a checklist, and let deterministic code apply the rules to those facts (Lyu et al., Jan 2023). Because code executes the rules directly on the recorded facts, the audit trail shows what truly produced the answer. This is Option 3 (Hybrid) in Table 3; Figure 4 walks through it on a worked prior-authorization request.

2. From "Black Box" to a Checkable System

In practice, internal model tracing hits a hard ceiling for both deployment models: proprietary APIs keep weights and layer activations behind the endpoint while exposing only condensed summaries, and open-weight models emit sampled tokens that can omit causal factors while full activation-level circuit tracing remains partial and costly.

Therefore, enterprise trust shifts from inspecting neural activations to engineering verifiable system controls around the model. "Black box" maps to nine distinct concerns. Each tends to come from a particular team and sits on a concrete business risk, so who is in the room usually signals which concerns matter first. Keep customer data inside enforceable boundaries, and screen every request at the edge.

Boundaries. Contractual zero-training on customer data, regional residency, VPC perimeters, customer-managed encryption keys (CMEK), and explicit retention bounds.

Input screening. Screen every request at the boundary for PII, toxicity, off-topic asks, and prompt injection (Episode 01 covers data boundaries and prompt-injection defenses for agents that reach databases).

Check on every request · Answers 1 Explainability, 6 Reliability

B · Ground in customer sources

Every answer cites the customer's own documents, or says it found nothing.

Customer gets: verbatim source quotes with links; an explicit "not found in your documents" when evidence is missing
On Google Cloud: RAG Engine grounding; structured output schemas

Pair retrieval with clickable inline citations linked to the customer's own documents, return structured JSON (verbatim_quote, sources, assumptions, answer), and return an explicit "not found in your documents" response whenever evidence is absent (Question 04 covers retrieval and citation design in depth).

Check on every request · Answers 1 Explainability, 2 Predictability

C · Check outputs in code

Code verifies every claim and applies the business rules, so the model never has the last word.

Customer gets: quote-verification results, rule checks, and schema validation
On Google Cloud: Model Armor output screening; your own service (for example, on Cloud Run) with structured output schemas

Verify in code. Server code checks that cited passages genuinely support each claim, screens outputs for PII and off-policy content, and enforces permissions, dollar caps, and business rules (Anthropic, Dec 2024).

Repeatability. Constrain outputs where repeatability matters (structured output schemas, enumerated values, and a pinned thinking level) while keeping sampling parameters at the defaults current reasoning models are tuned for (Gemini 3 guide, 2026). No decoding setting makes a hosted model repeatable: even greedy decoding on shared inference servers varies with the batch a request lands in (in one study, 1,000 identical requests to an open-weight model produced 80 distinct outputs; He et al., Sep 2025). Promise measured consistency (pass^k), and put calculations, thresholds, and final decisions in code.

Check on every request · Answers 6 Reliability, 7 Accountability

D · Route by risk

Low-risk cases run automatically; uncertain or high-impact ones go to a named reviewer.

Customer gets: named reviewer sign-off; the routing threshold and its measured precision
On Google Cloud: the customer's review queue, with sign-offs logged to Cloud Logging

Route by error cost. Automate low-risk tasks, and route uncertain or high-impact cases, with dry-run previews, to named human reviewers with clear sign-off ownership (RACI: responsible, accountable, consulted, informed).

Calibrate the signal. A model's self-reported confidence ("I am 95% sure") tends to be overconfident (Xiong et al., ICLR 2024), so route on evidence the system can check: whether every cited quote was found in the source, whether every rule criterion resolved, agreement across k independent samples (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), and token log-probabilities where the API exposes them. Tune the routing threshold on the golden set to hit a target precision for auto-approved cases (for example, at least 99% agreement with labels from subject-matter experts (SMEs)), and send everything below it to review.

Prevent rubber-stamping. Show reviewers the evidence before the model's recommendation, track each reviewer's override rate, and occasionally insert known-bad cases to confirm reviews are real.

Check on every request · Answers 7 Accountability

E · Log every step

Every input, source, model version, check, and sign-off is recorded, so any decision can be replayed.

Customer gets: a trace ID that replays any decision
On Google Cloud: Cloud Logging, Cloud Trace

What to log. Every interaction: input, retrieved chunks, prompt version, pinned model version, thought summaries as supporting evidence, output, and reviewer feedback; record step-by-step traces across every tool call so any decision can be reconstructed months later, and expose live drift and quality dashboards to operators.

Provider errors. Treat provider errors as part of observability: isolate HTTP 429 and timeout faults into retry queues with backoff, and record each retry in the trace so a reconstructed run shows which calls failed and why.

Control on every change · Answers 2 Predictability, 8 Version Stability, 9 Quality

F · Evaluate and pin

A golden set built with domain experts gates every change, and the model version moves only when it passes.

Customer gets: golden-set results, pass^k consistency (a case passes only if all k runs succeed; Yao et al., ICLR 2025), and failure slices; a pinned model ID and regression report before each cutover
On Google Cloud: Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (Agent Platform) evaluation service; pinned model version IDs; evaluation pipeline in CI

Golden set. Co-create a golden set of real historical cases alongside the customer's domain specialists, sized to the workflow's risk and edge-case coverage; track accuracy, groundedness, hallucination rate, refusal rate, latency, and cost; rerun the set like a regression suite on every prompt, retrieval, or model change; and share the failure breakdown openly. Where an LLM judge scores a metric, first measure the judge's agreement with SME labels on a held-out slice.

Pin versions. Upgrade only when the customer's golden set passes. Pinning is temporary: providers retire API model versions on a published schedule, so maintain a migration calendar and run each successor through the customer's golden set before cutover. Self-hosted open weights (Option 4) remove forced retirement altogether.

Training caution. If you fine-tune or run reinforcement learning, keep the reasoning trace out of the reward signal so it stays useful for monitoring (Baker et al., Mar 2025).

Control on every change · Answers 6 Reliability

G · Roll out gradually

Shadow mode first, automation only when the numbers clear the bar, with a kill switch.

Customer gets: shadow-mode comparison results; a kill switch
On Google Cloud: Agent Platform evaluation service for shadow comparisons; a feature flag in the serving layer

Start on a low-risk, high-visibility wedge and earn promotion across stages: Scoped Pilot → Shadow Mode (running silently beside humans to compare outcomes) → Assisted Copilot → Partial Scoped Automation, backed by an instant operational kill switch (Hodgson, Oct 2017).

3. Keep the Decision Out of the Model

Section 2's layers check the model's work. The stronger move is to give the model less to decide. When a model pulls a fact and its quote from a document, code can confirm the quote is in the source. When a model makes the decision itself, its reasons stay in activations no one can read (Section 1). So split each decision step: the model does the language work, and rules a person can read make the decision. One worked case shows why it matters.

The case. Prior authorization is a health plan's approval before it pays for a procedure: the doctor submits the patient's chart, and the plan checks it against a written medical policy. Plans already use AI here, under a firm rule: software may approve, but only a licensed clinician may deny for medical necessity. California's SB 1120 writes that into law, Medicare's WISeR pilot reserves denials for clinicians, and CMS requires Medicare Advantage decisions to rest on the individual patient's record. A compliance lead asks: "If your assistant recommends denying a lumbar MRI, can we show the member and the regulator exactly why?" Imaging policies typically cover a lumbar MRI for low back pain when either (a) at least six weeks of conservative treatment in the last six months has failed, or (b) a new or worsening neurological deficit is documented on exam (NIA Clinical Guideline NIA_CG_044).

When the model's reason decides. The model reads the chart and the policy, recommends DENY, and its thinking reads "patient only tried ibuprofen for 3 weeks." A clinician working through a long queue sees a fluent reason and signs. The denial is wrong: nobody checked criterion (b). A clinician in the loop does not catch it, because what they review is the model's story, and the thinking reads as a complete reason whether or not the model checked (b). The process is compliant: a licensed clinician made the call. It is still wrong.

When code checks. The model only extracts facts, each with a verbatim quote:

Extracted Chart Facts with Verbatim Quotes (Model Output)
{
  "conservative_therapy": {
    "type": "over-the-counter ibuprofen",
    "weeks": 3,
    "quote": "Ibuprofen 400 mg TID x 3 weeks, minimal relief"
  },
  "neuro_deficit": {"documented": null, "quote": null}
}

Code confirms each quote exists in the chart, then applies the policy: (a) is not met (three weeks against a six-week minimum), and (b) has no evidence either way. Code approves automatically only when a criterion is met with a verified quote, and it never denies. Here it requests the neurological exam notes and, if they do not settle (b), routes the case to a clinician with the evidence laid out criterion by criterion (Figure 4).

What the member and the auditor get. If the clinician denies, the notice is drafted from an approved template and signed by that clinician, with every clause traced to a quote: "Denied under [policy ID, version]. The policy covers a lumbar MRI after at least six weeks of conservative treatment or when a new or worsening neurological deficit is documented. Your records show three weeks of over-the-counter ibuprofen and no neurological deficit in the exam notes provided." The auditor gets the same facts, each with its quote and character offsets, the policy version, the pinned model version, a trace ID that replays the run (Layer E), and the clinician's sign-off (Layer D). The model's thinking is stored beside them as supporting evidence.

Six ways to split the work. The case mixes several splits, one per step: finding the policy is pure search, extracting quoted facts and applying the rule is the hybrid, the six-week threshold is a deterministic rule, and the notice fills an approved template. Table 3 lists the six options. For high-stakes decisions made from documents, the hybrid is the default.

4. Summary: Putting It Together

Every LLM—whether proprietary or open-weight—stores knowledge across billions of weights and computes per-request activations that are discarded after each token. Thought summaries, raw thinking tokens, and research-grade circuit tracing provide partial visibility, while leaving the causal reason behind a specific answer unverified. Enterprise trust therefore comes from the engineered system surrounding the model.

Building that trust starts by identifying which of the nine "black box" concerns (Table 1) and underlying risks drives the requirement, then wrapping the model in seven verifiable layers (A–G), five checks on every request and two controls on every change: a governed data perimeter and provenance record (A), source-grounded citations (B), deterministic output verification and rules in code (C), risk-based routing to accountable reviewers (D), replayable audit logs (E), co-owned golden-set evaluation and version pinning (F), and graduated rollout from shadow mode behind a kill switch (G). Applicable regulations and error cost decide which layers come first.

Alongside those layers, architecture limits how much of each decision the model makes at all. Choosing per step among the six options (Table 3)—especially the hybrid pattern where the model extracts quoted facts and deterministic code executes the policy rule—ensures that high-stakes outcomes are both explainable and defensible. On Google Cloud's Agent Platform, those layers map directly to VPC Service Controls, CMEK, and Model Armor for perimeter and screening, RAG Engine grounding over customer data stores, Cloud Run for deterministic rule checks, Cloud Logging and Cloud Trace for replay, and the Agent Platform evaluation service with explicit model version pinning.

Likely Follow-Up Questions

  • "Can't we just make the model deterministic?" Yes, through structure and code; decoding settings alone leave run-to-run variation. Current reasoning models such as Gemini 3 are tuned to run at the default sampling temperature, and Google advises against lowering it; even greedy decoding on shared servers varies with batching. We get predictability from schemas and enumerated outputs, pinned versions and thinking levels, measured pass^k on the customer's golden set, and code for every step that must give the same answer every time.
  • "Why not show the model's chain of thought to the auditor?" Reasoning traces can omit what drove the answer; in hint-based tests, reasoning models often disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases. We log reasoning traces for debugging and give auditors verified quotes, the rules that ran, and a replayable run log.
  • "Doesn't an open-weight model solve the black-box problem?" It solves access, while leaving per-decision understanding unsolved. Open weights let the customer pin, inspect, and self-host the exact checkpoint, but explaining an individual decision from those weights remains research-grade; explanations come from the system layers.
  • "How do you decide what gets auto-approved?" On checkable signals (every quote found, every criterion resolved, agreement across samples) rather than the model's self-reported confidence. The threshold is tuned on the golden set to a target precision, and everything below it goes to a named reviewer.
  • "What happens when the provider retires our pinned model?" Pinning buys time: we keep a migration calendar, run each successor through the customer's golden set, and cut over only when it passes. Self-hosted open weights (Option 4) remove forced retirement altogether.
  • "Isn't an LLM judge in your evaluation just another black box?" It is, until it is calibrated. We measure the judge's agreement with SME labels on a held-out slice, publish that agreement rate, and use the judge only for metrics where it meets the bar.

Common mistakes to avoid:

  • Presenting the reasoning trace as the explanation for a decision.
  • Routing on the model's self-reported confidence.
  • Using an LLM judge without measuring its agreement with experts.
  • Pinning a model version without a migration plan.
  • Building the golden set from synthetic cases alone instead of real historical ones.
  • Treating human review as a safeguard without measuring whether reviewers ever disagree with the model.
Takeaway: Diagnose which of the nine "black box" concerns applies, be candid that model weights and activations stay opaque, wrap the model in seven transparent system layers (data perimeter, grounding in customer sources, output checks in code, risk-based routing, step-level logging, golden-set evaluation with version pinning, and gradual rollout), use hybrid designs where code makes high-stakes calls on verified facts, and treat reasoning traces as supporting evidence.
