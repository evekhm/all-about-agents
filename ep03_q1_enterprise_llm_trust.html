Episode 03 · AI System Design Interviews · Question 01

Making an LLM Trustworthy for an Enterprise Customer

How far we can see into a model's reasoning traces, what "black box" means to a customer beyond latent activations, and the system layers that make an LLM solution transparent.

My goal with this question is to answer one of the most common questions in enterprise AI system-design interviews: how to make a language model trustworthy to a skeptical customer. We start inside the model—what reasoning traces exist, what granularity each reaches, and why open weights still require multi-step instrumentation—before stepping outside to the nine stakeholder concerns, seven transparent system layers, and six architectural alternatives.

The 30-second answer: Every LLM, open or closed, is opaque at the level of its weights and activations, and its reasoning traces are unverified as proof of why it answered. Trust comes from building an engineered system around the model so we can control and verify it even while its internals stay opaque. Ground every answer in cited sources, and measure quality and consistency on a golden set. Log every step so any decision can be replayed, and enforce deterministic code guardrails with pinned model versions. Let code make high-stakes decisions on verified facts, route uncertain cases to human review, and roll out gradually from shadow mode to automation behind an instant kill switch.

What we will walk through step by step:

  1. Inside the model: why weights and activations are opaque for both APIs and open weights, and the three levels of reasoning-trace granularity.
  2. What "black box" means: the nine distinct stakeholder concerns and six business risks that matter outside the weights.
  3. Seven system layers: the engineering controls (A–G) and Google Cloud services that address each concern.
  4. Scoping, regulations, and six alternatives: how scoping questions, regulatory rules, and cost determine which controls and architectural options to apply.
  5. Worked example: tracing a prior-authorization request end to end to compare a trace-based denial against a verifiable hybrid design, followed by a spoken summary and likely follow-up questions.

Calling a language model a "black box" points to four general engineering requirements: behavioral predictability, output verifiability, enforceable boundaries, and defensibility under audit and regulatory scrutiny. Understanding how to meet those requirements begins with the model itself—specifically, which internal reasoning traces are observable, the granularity each trace exposes, and where the boundary of opaque weights and latent activations lies.

1. Tracing a Model's Reasoning: What You Can and Cannot See

You can read what a model writes while it reasons, but not reliably why it reached its answer. This section explains where reasoning actually happens, what each kind of trace shows, and what each one can prove.

Weights and activations. A model works with two kinds of numbers. Weights are the billions of values learned during training. They are fixed, identical for every request, and hold the model's knowledge with no human-readable if-then rules. Activations are the numbers the model computes from those weights and your input while it handles one request: its working state, which exists only during that request. In the formula y = 2x + 3, the 2 and the 3 play the role of weights; with x = 5, the intermediate 10 and the result 13 play the role of activations. This is the same for every LLM, whether it runs behind a proprietary API or on open weights. The difference is only who can see the numbers.

One token at a time. A model writes its answer one token, a word or part of a word, at a time. For each token it runs one forward pass: the input and everything written so far flow up through every layer, each layer computes new activations, and the last layer turns them into probabilities for the next token. One token is sampled, and the rest of that pass's activations are discarded. Each layer's keys and values are cached for speed, but they can be rebuilt from the tokens. The new token is appended to the input, and the next pass begins. Figure 1 shows four steps of this loop on the prior-authorization case from Section 5.

Figure 1 — One forward pass per token: activations are computed through every layer and discarded once the next token is sampled, and only the written token is fed back as the next input. Later passes reuse earlier tokens' cached keys and values (KV cache), but those are recomputable from the tokens, so the written trace is the only state carried between steps. A separate pass condenses the raw trace into a thought summary.

What this means. Everything the model weighs while choosing a token lives in activations that are never written down. The only thing carried from one step to the next is the written token. The written reasoning is the model's notepad, not a recording of its thinking.

Three things you can look at (the three levels in Figure 1):

  • Level 1 · Thought summaries. What you see: a short prose summary of the model's reasoning. Who can see it: anyone using a reasoning API such as Gemini (Google, 2026); this is usually all a proprietary API returns. What it proves: little about why the model answered as it did. A summary sits two steps removed from the computation: it condenses tokens that were themselves sampled from activations (Figure 1).
  • Level 2 · Raw thinking tokens. What you see: the actual tokens the model wrote to itself before answering. Who can see it: users of open-weight reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1; proprietary APIs usually keep them with the provider. What it proves: what the model wrote, not what caused its answer. On hard multi-step problems, the model has to write intermediate results down to use them in later steps, which makes the trace useful working memory to monitor (Korbak et al., Jul 2025). But it can leave out the real reason, as the hint test below shows.
  • Level 3 · Activations (circuit tracing). What you see: which internal features fired and how they pushed the model toward its next token. Who can see it: only teams running open weights on infrastructure they control, with specialist tooling. What it proves: part of the real internal path for one prompt. Attribution graphs (Lindsey et al., Mar 2025)—brought to open-weight models by the circuit-tracing library—and sparse autoencoders (Gemma Scope 2) capture only a fraction of the computation, at research cost.
Example: the hint test. Researchers ask a model a multiple-choice question with a hint added, such as "A Stanford professor indicates the answer is (A)," pointing to an answer the model would not otherwise pick. When the hint changes the answer, they check whether the model's reasoning mentions it. Often it does not: the trace builds a plausible argument for (A), like a student who saw the answer key and writes out a full solution without mentioning it. In these tests, reasoning models often disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases (Chen et al., May 2025), extending earlier results on prompted chain-of-thought (Turpin et al., NeurIPS 2023). Newer models follow planted hints less often, which makes this test harder to run, but it does not make their traces proof.

The practical rule: save reasoning traces (raw thinking tokens or thought summaries) in your audit logs so engineers can debug and monitor the model, and base any customer-facing or regulatory justification on artifacts that code can verify, such as exact source quotes, database records, and explicit rule checks. Research models that reason directly in continuous latent space (Hao et al., Dec 2024) skip written tokens altogether, which would remove Level 2 entirely.

What it takes to look inside (Level 3). Downloading open weights does not explain anything by itself: the checkpoint is a static archive of trained numbers. Explaining one prompt takes three engineering steps:

  1. Record the activations. Host the model on infrastructure you control and attach hooks that save each layer's activations as the prompt runs.
  2. Split them into readable features. A single activation value usually mixes many unrelated concepts, so a second, specially trained model, a sparse autoencoder or transcoder, splits the activations into features a person can try to label. Each must be trained for that exact model.
  3. Trace how features influence each other. Link the active features across layers into an attribution graph showing which ones pushed the final token up or down. This is an expensive, research-grade investigation that still captures only part of the computation.

The way out: make the written reasoning the computation. A fourth route avoids the faithfulness problem altogether: have the model translate the case into a checklist or program that deterministic code executes, so the written reasoning is the computation that produced the answer (Lyu et al., Jan 2023). This is Option 3 (Hybrid) in Table 2; Section 5 and Figure 4 walk through it on a real prior-authorization request.

2. Beyond Latent Activations: What "Black Box" Means to a Customer

In practice, internal model tracing hits a hard ceiling for both deployment models: proprietary APIs keep weights and layer activations behind the endpoint while exposing only condensed summaries, and open-weight models emit sampled tokens that can omit causal factors while full activation-level circuit tracing remains partial and costly.

Therefore, enterprise trust shifts from inspecting neural activations to engineering verifiable system controls around the model (Section 3). "Black box" maps to nine distinct concerns (Table 1), and who is in the room usually signals which ones matter first:

  • End users want sources behind an answer.
  • Engineering wants predictable outputs and version stability.
  • Risk and legal want to know what data the model learned from and whether anyone can inspect its weights.
  • Security wants data boundaries.
  • Compliance wants an audit trail.

Underneath those nine angles sit six concrete business and personal risks that decide whether a project moves forward:

  • Legal and Financial Liability: A wrong answer reaches an external customer, a patient record, or a financial ledger, and the enterprise owns the consequences.
  • Regulatory Scrutiny: Frameworks such as the EU AI Act, GDPR automated-decision rules, banking model risk management (SR 26-2, which replaced SR 11-7), HIPAA, and FDA guidance require defensible explanations when an examiner asks how a decision was reached.
  • Confidential Data Exposure: Proprietary records leaking into vendor training runs or surfacing across users via prompt injection.
  • Reputational Harm: A public screenshot of an assistant stating something fabricated or off-policy.
  • Loss of Control and Lock-In: Unplanned vendor shifts in model behavior, terms, or unit pricing.
  • Personal Career Risk for the Internal Champion: The sponsor backing the project carries personal accountability if an opaque failure embarrasses their team.

That gives us the governing boundary: the model's weights and latent activations remain opaque, while the surrounding system makes inputs, sources, limits, evaluations, and history transparent (Figure 2).

3. Seven System Layers Around the Model

The nine concerns in Table 1 are covered by seven engineering layers around the model (Table 3 maps each concern to its layer, the evidence a customer receives, and the Google Cloud service that produces it):

  • A · Ground Outputs in Evidence (Explainability): Pair retrieval with clickable inline citations linked to the customer's own documents, return structured JSON (verbatim_quote, sources, assumptions, answer), verify in server code that cited passages genuinely support the claim, and return an explicit "not found in your documents" response whenever evidence is absent (Question 04 covers retrieval and citation design in depth).
  • B · Measure It Openly with Customer Experts (Evaluation): Co-create a golden set of 100 to 300 real historical cases alongside the customer's domain specialists; track accuracy, groundedness, hallucination rate, refusal rate, latency, and cost; rerun the set like a regression suite on every prompt, retrieval, or model change; and share the failure breakdown openly ("94% pass rate on your golden set; the remaining 6% fall into these boundary categories, which route to review"). Slice results by the segments that matter for fairness and coverage (region, product line, and protected attributes where legally permitted) so a high overall pass rate cannot hide a failing group. After launch, sample a fixed share of production cases each week for SME review so the golden set keeps pace with real traffic.
  • C · Observe Every Step (Auditability): Log every interaction (input, retrieved chunks, prompt version, pinned model version, thought summaries as supporting evidence, output, and reviewer feedback), record step-by-step traces across every tool call so any decision can be reconstructed months later, and expose live drift and quality dashboards to operators. Treat provider errors as part of observability: isolate HTTP 429 and timeout faults into retry queues with backoff, and record each retry in the trace so a reconstructed run shows which calls failed and why.
  • D · Enforce Deterministic Guardrails and Version Pinning (Control & Stability): Screen inputs and outputs for PII, toxicity, off-topic asks, and prompt injection (Episode 01 covers data boundaries and prompt-injection defenses for agents that reach databases), and enforce permissions, dollar caps, and business rules in application code (Anthropic, Dec 2024).

    Constrain outputs where repeatability matters (structured output schemas, enumerated values, and a pinned thinking level) while keeping sampling parameters at the defaults current reasoning models are tuned for (Google, 2026). No decoding setting makes a hosted model repeatable: even greedy decoding on shared inference servers varies with the batch a request lands in (in one study, 1,000 identical requests to an open-weight model produced 80 distinct outputs; He et al., Sep 2025). Promise measured consistency (pass^k), and put calculations, thresholds, and final decisions in code.

    If you fine-tune or run reinforcement learning, keep the reasoning trace out of the reward signal so it stays useful for monitoring (Baker et al., Mar 2025). Pin exact model versions so upgrades occur only when the customer's golden set passes. Pinning is temporary: providers retire API model versions on a published schedule, so maintain a migration calendar and run each successor through the customer's golden set before cutover. Self-hosted open weights (Option 4) remove forced retirement altogether.

  • E · Keep Accountable Humans in the Loop (Accountability): Match autonomy to error cost by automating low-risk tasks while routing uncertain or high-impact cases via calibrated routing thresholds and dry-run previews to named human reviewers with clear sign-off ownership (RACI). Calibrated thresholds need a calibrated signal. A model's self-reported confidence ("I am 95% sure") tends to be overconfident (Xiong et al., ICLR 2024), so route on evidence the system can check: whether every cited quote was found in the source, whether every rule criterion resolved, agreement across k independent samples (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), and token log-probabilities where the API exposes them. Tune the routing threshold on the golden set to hit a target precision for auto-approved cases (for example, at least 99% agreement with SME labels), and send everything below it to review. Guard against rubber-stamping: show reviewers the evidence before the model's recommendation, track each reviewer's override rate, and occasionally insert known-bad cases to confirm reviews are real.
  • F · Document Model and Data Provenance (Governance): Provide model cards (intended scope, limitations, benchmark results), compliance certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001 / 42001, HIPAA BAA), and enforceable cloud data guarantees: contractual zero-training on customer data, regional residency, VPC perimeters, customer-managed encryption keys (CMEK), and explicit retention bounds.
  • G · Roll Out Gradually (Earned Trust): Start on a low-risk, high-visibility wedge and earn promotion across stages: Scoped Pilot → Shadow Mode (running silently beside humans to compare outcomes) → Assisted Copilot → Partial Scoped Automation, backed by an instant operational kill switch (Hodgson, Oct 2017).

4. Scoping Questions and the Six Alternatives

How far we go on those controls depends on seven questions we align on with the customer:

  1. What exact decision does the system make, and what does an error cost?
  2. Who needs the explanation? End users need citations, operators need dashboards, auditors need logs and lineage, and regulators need formal documentation.
  3. Which regulations apply? (Table 4)
  4. Where does the data live, and who can access it?
  5. What measurable bar defines "good" before we build?
  6. How fast is a bad output detected and fixed?
  7. How do model updates roll out?

Depending on the answers, we choose for each step among six options that trade open-ended language flexibility against structural transparency (Table 2, Figure 3):

Table 2 · Six Options Trading Transparency Against Flexibility
Architectural Option Transparency & Strengths Flexibility Trade-Off & Best Fit
1. Deterministic Rules & Solvers 100% inspectable if-then logic, SQL, or math solvers Requires structured inputs; ideal for calculations, thresholds, & hard policy gates
2. Interpretable Classical ML + SHAP Decision trees, logistic regression, or GBDTs with per-feature SHAP attributions Tabular inputs; ideal for regulated credit scoring, risk grading, & numerical forecasting
3. Hybrid Architecture (Sweet Spot) LLM extracts structured fields or drafts; deterministic rules or scoring models decide Combines unstructured language understanding with 100% auditable final decision logic
4. Pinned Small / Open-Weight Models Frozen weight hashes, customer-controlled hosting, & full data/checkpoint provenance Weights remain neural parameters; ideal when strict version immutability or air-gapping is required
5. Constrained Generation Classifies into fixed enums or selects from pre-approved legal/support templates Eliminates open-ended phrasing risk while keeping natural-language intent understanding
6. Pure Search & Retrieval Surfaces ranked original source passages directly to the human reader Zero synthesis hallucination risk when users prefer reading primary clauses themselves

The Cost of Trust. Each layer has a price: citation verification adds a retrieval and a check to every answer, k-sample agreement multiplies inference cost by k, and human review adds minutes of expert time per routed case. Matching autonomy to error cost (Layer E) is what keeps that price bounded: low-risk, high-volume cases run fully automated with sampled audits, while the expensive controls concentrate on the small share of decisions where a mistake is costly.

5. Worked Example: Explaining a Prior-Authorization Decision

A health plan's compliance lead asks the interview question in its sharpest form: "If your assistant denies a lumbar MRI, can we show the member and the regulator exactly why?" Since 2026, the CMS prior-authorization rule has required a specific reason for every denial, so this is a hard requirement.

Diagnose first. The concerns are Explainability and Accountability (Table 1); the risks are Regulatory Scrutiny and Legal Liability. That points to Layers A, C, and E, and to Option 3 (Hybrid) for the decision itself.

The risky design. The model reads the chart and the policy, returns DENY, and its thinking reads "patient only tried ibuprofen for 3 weeks." The system stores that text as the denial reason. Two things are wrong. First, the trace is unverified as proof of what drove the answer (Section 1). Second, the decision itself is wrong: the policy approves the MRI when either (a) six weeks of supervised physical therapy failed or (b) a progressive neurological deficit is documented, and nobody checked (b).

The verifiable design. The model's only job is extraction. It returns structured facts, each with a verbatim quote from the chart:

Extracted Chart Facts with Verbatim Quotes (Model Output)
{
  "cpt_code": "72148",
  "conservative_therapy": {
    "type": "OTC NSAID (ibuprofen)",
    "supervised_pt": false,
    "weeks": 3,
    "quote": "Ibuprofen 400 mg TID x 3 weeks, minimal relief"
  },
  "neuro_deficit": {"documented": null, "quote": null}
}

Code then does everything that needs to be defensible: it confirms each quote exists in the chart, applies the written policy rule, and routes the result (Figure 4 and Table 5).

When the model decides, its thinking becomes the stored reason and criterion (b) is never checked, so the result is a wrongful denial. What the auditor receives. The verified facts and their source quotes, the policy rule and its version, the pinned model version, a trace ID that replays the run (Layer C), and the reviewer's sign-off if the case was routed (Layer E). The model's thinking summary is stored beside them as supporting evidence.

Why it matters. The verifiable design does more than explain the decision; it changed it. A plausible-sounding trace would have produced a wrongful denial. Code evaluating the actual rule produced a request for the missing evidence. Question 05 builds the full policy agent around this pattern.

6. Summary: Putting It Together in One Answer

"Every LLM is opaque at the weight and activation level, so we treat reasoning traces as debugging evidence and build trust in the surrounding system. We first identify which of the nine concerns and six risks drives the customer's objection. Then we wrap the model in seven layers: cited grounding, a co-owned golden set, replayable audit logs, code guardrails and version pinning, risk-matched human review, contractual data governance, and a shadow-to-automation rollout with a kill switch. For high-stakes steps, we choose the right one of our six alternatives so deterministic rules or classical ML make final calls on verified facts."

On Google Cloud's Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (Agent Platform), those layers map directly to RAG Engine grounding over customer data stores, the Agent Platform evaluation service, Cloud Logging and Cloud Trace, Model Armor input/output screening, VPC Service Controls and CMEK for perimeter and key ownership, and explicit model version pinning.

Likely Follow-Up Questions

  • "Can't we just make the model deterministic?" Through structure and code, yes; through a decoding setting, no. Current reasoning models such as Gemini 3 are tuned to run at their default sampling temperature, and Google advises against lowering it; even greedy decoding on shared servers varies with batching. We get predictability from schemas and enumerated outputs, pinned versions and thinking levels, measured pass^k on the customer's golden set, and code for every step that must give the same answer every time.
  • "Why not show the model's chain of thought to the auditor?" Reasoning traces often omit what actually drove the answer; in hint-based tests, reasoning models often disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases. We log traces for debugging and give auditors verified quotes, rules, and replayable traces.
  • "Doesn't an open-weight model solve the black-box problem?" It solves access, while leaving per-decision understanding unsolved. Open weights let the customer pin, inspect, and self-host the exact checkpoint, and explaining an individual decision from those weights remains research-grade; explanations come from the system layers.
  • "How do you decide what gets auto-approved?" On checkable signals (every quote found, every criterion resolved, agreement across samples) rather than the model's self-reported confidence. The threshold is tuned on the golden set to a target precision, and everything below it goes to a named reviewer.
  • "What happens when the provider retires our pinned model?" Pinning buys time: we keep a migration calendar, run each successor through the customer's golden set, and cut over only when it passes. Self-hosted open weights (Option 4) remove forced retirement altogether.
  • "Isn't an LLM judge in your evaluation just another black box?" It is unless calibrated. We measure the judge's agreement with SME labels on a held-out slice, publish that agreement rate, and use the judge only for metrics where it meets the bar.

Common mistakes to avoid:

  • Presenting the reasoning trace as the explanation for a decision.
  • Routing on the model's self-reported confidence.
  • Using an LLM judge without measuring its agreement with experts.
  • Pinning a model version without a migration plan.
  • Building the golden set from synthetic cases alone instead of real historical ones.
  • Treating human review as a safeguard without measuring whether reviewers ever disagree with the model.
Takeaway: Diagnose which of the nine "black box" concerns applies, be candid that model weights and activations stay opaque, wrap the model in seven transparent system layers (grounding, co-owned evals, audit traces, code guardrails + version pinning, risk-matched HITL, provenance, and phased shadow-to-auto rollout), use hybrid designs where code makes high-stakes calls on verified facts, and treat reasoning traces as supporting evidence.
