Episode 03 · AI System Design Interviews · Question 01

Making an LLM Trustworthy for an Enterprise Customer

How far we can see into a model's reasoning traces, what "black box" means to a customer beyond latent activations, and the system layers that make an LLM solution transparent.

My goal here is to answer one of the most common questions in enterprise AI system-design interviews: how to make a language model trustworthy to a skeptical customer. We start inside the model—what reasoning traces exist and what each one can prove—before stepping outside to the nine stakeholder concerns, the seven system layers that answer them, and keeping high-stakes decisions out of the model.

The short answer. Neither proprietary APIs nor open weights give you a production-grade proof of why a model gave a specific answer: thought summaries condense, raw traces can omit causal factors, and circuit tracing remains partial research. Leave the model opaque and make everything around it checkable: the data it sees, the sources it cites, the rules in code, the people who sign off, and a record of every step. For high-stakes decisions, let the model read and let code decide.

What we will walk through step by step:

  1. Inside the model: why weights and activations stay opaque for both APIs and open weights, and the three levels of reasoning traces you can see.
  2. From "black box" to a checkable system: the nine stakeholder concerns and the business risks behind them, and the seven layers (A–G) with the Google Cloud services that address each.
  3. Keep the decision out of the model: the model reads and code decides, shown on a worked prior-authorization example.
  4. Summary and follow-up questions: spoken wrap-up and likely interview follow-ups.

1. Tracing a Model's Reasoning: What You Can and Cannot See

You can read what a model writes while it reasons, but not reliably why it reached its answer. This section explains where reasoning actually happens, what each kind of trace shows, and what each one can prove.

Weights and activations. A model works with two kinds of numbers. Weights are the billions of values learned during training. They are fixed and identical for every request. The model's knowledge is spread across all of them at once, so there is no rule or line you can point to and read. Activations are the numbers the model computes from those weights and your input while it handles one request: its working state, which exists only during that request. In the formula y = 2x + 3, the 2 and the 3 play the role of weights; with x = 5, the intermediate 10 and the result 13 play the role of activations. This is the same for every LLM, whether it runs behind a proprietary API or on open weights. The difference is only who can see the numbers.

One token at a time. A model writes its answer one token, a word or part of a word, at a time. For each token it runs one forward pass: the input and everything written so far flow up through every layer, each layer computes new activations, and the last layer turns them into probabilities for the next token. One token is sampled, and that pass's activations are discarded. Each layer's keys and values are cached for speed, but they can be rebuilt from the tokens. The new token is appended to the input, and the next pass begins. Figure 1 shows four steps of this loop on the prior-authorization case from Section 3: after reading "ibuprofen," the model writes "needs," "6 wk," "PT" (physical therapy), and "fail."

What this means. Everything the model weighs while choosing a token lives in activations that are never written down. The only thing carried from one step to the next is the text so far: the prompt plus every token the model has generated. That generated text is the model's notepad; the thinking behind each token stays in the discarded activations.

Three things you can look at (the three levels in Figure 1):

  • Level 1 · Thought summaries. What you see: a short prose summary of the model's reasoning. Who can see it: anyone using a reasoning API such as the Gemini API (Gemini API thinking docs, 2026); this is usually all a proprietary API returns. What it proves: little about why the model answered as it did. A summary sits two steps removed from the computation: it condenses tokens that were themselves sampled from activations.
  • Level 2 · Raw thinking tokens. What you see: the actual tokens the model wrote to itself before answering. Who can see it: users of open-weight reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek Thinking Mode, which exposes the full reasoning trace in reasoning_content or <think> tags); proprietary APIs usually keep them with the provider. What it proves: what the model wrote; the cause of its answer stays out of view. On hard multi-step problems, the model has to write intermediate results down to use them in later steps, which makes the trace useful working memory to monitor (Korbak et al., Jul 2025). But it can leave out the real reason, as the hint test below shows.
  • Level 3 · Activations (circuit tracing). What you see: which internal concepts ("features") fired and how they wired together across layers—like tracing signals on a circuit board—to push the model toward its next token. Who can see it: only teams running open weights on infrastructure they control, with specialist tooling. What it proves: part of the real internal path for one prompt. Attribution graphs (Lindsey et al., Mar 2025)—brought to open-weight models by the circuit-tracing library—and sparse autoencoders (Gemma Scope 2) capture only a fraction of the computation, at research cost.
Example: the hint test. Researchers ask a model a multiple-choice question with a hint added, such as "A Stanford professor indicates the answer is (A)," pointing to an answer the model would not otherwise pick. When the hint changes the answer, they check whether the model's reasoning mentions it. Frequently it does not: the trace builds a plausible argument for (A), like a student who saw the answer key and writes out a full solution without mentioning it. In these tests, reasoning models disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases (Chen et al., May 2025), extending earlier results on prompted chain-of-thought (Turpin et al., NeurIPS 2023). Newer models follow planted hints less often, which makes this test harder to run, but it does not make the traces proof.

What it takes to look inside (Level 3). Downloading open weights does not explain anything by itself: the checkpoint is a static archive of trained numbers. Explaining one prompt takes three engineering steps:

  1. Record the activations. Host the model on infrastructure you control and attach hooks that save each layer's activations as the prompt runs.
  2. Split them into readable features. A single activation value usually mixes many unrelated concepts, so a second, specially trained model, a sparse autoencoder or transcoder, splits the activations into features a person can try to label. Each one has to be trained for that exact model.
  3. Trace how features influence each other. Link the active features across layers into an attribution graph showing which ones pushed the final token up or down. This is an expensive, research-grade investigation that still captures only part of the computation.

Where that leaves the customer. Neither you nor the provider can reliably audit a model's internal reasons in production: summaries condense, thinking tokens can leave out the real cause, and circuit tracing is partial research. Leave the model unexplained; make everything around it checkable (Section 2), and keep decisions that matter out of it (Section 3).

2. From "Black Box" to a Checkable System

Since internal model reasons remain unverified in production, check everything else. To a customer, "black box" means nine different concerns. Each tends to come from a particular team and carries a business risk, so who is in the room tells you which concerns come first. Answers 3 Training-Data Provenance, 5 Data Governance

A · Data perimeter

Keep customer data inside enforceable boundaries, and screen every request at the edge.

Customer gets: zero-training terms, residency region, key ownership, and input-screening results; model card, data statements, and certifications
On Google Cloud: VPC Service Controls, customer-managed encryption keys (CMEK, in Cloud KMS), IAM Principal Access Boundaries (PAB), and Agent Gateway with Model Armor + Sensitive Data Protection (Cloud SDP) (full wiring in Episode 01); published model cards

Boundaries. Contractual zero-training on customer data, regional residency, VPC perimeters, CMEK, and explicit retention bounds.

Model documentation. For training-data questions, provide the model card, data statements (source categories, knowledge cut-off, filtering), licensing terms, and IP indemnification.

Input screening. Screen every request at the boundary for PII, toxicity, off-topic asks, and prompt injection. Screening lowers attack volume; the boundary that holds is the code check in C · Check outputs in code and the data-access rules of Episode 01.

Answers 1 Explainability, 6 Reliability

B · Ground in customer sources

Every answer cites the customer's own documents, or says it found nothing.

Customer gets: verbatim source quotes with links; an explicit "not found in your documents" when evidence is missing
On Google Cloud: Agent Search, RAG Engine, or Agent Retrieval / Vector Search (Agent Platform); or in-place BigQuery / AlloyDB vector search, returning verifiable source URIs for clickable citations

Pair retrieval with clickable inline citations linked to the customer's own documents, return structured JSON (verbatim_quote, sources, assumptions, answer), and return an explicit "not found in your documents" response whenever evidence is absent. Retrieval runs under the caller's verified identity, so citations never expose documents the caller cannot open (Episode 01).

Choosing a grounding backend. Picking between Agent Search, BigQuery / AlloyDB vector search, Agent Retrieval, and RAG Engine depends on data residency, latency, and chunking needs; Question 04 covers retrieval and citation design.

Answers 1 Explainability, 2 Predictability

C · Check outputs in code

Code verifies every claim and applies the business rules, so the model never has the last word.

Customer gets: quote-verification results, rule checks, and schema validation
On Google Cloud: Model Armor reply screening and Cloud SDP de-identification at Agent Gateway; Agent Registry (catalog) + Agent Gateway IAM and Identity-Aware Proxy (IAP) (tool-call authorization); Cloud Run service enforcing structured output schemas and deterministic rules

Verify in code. Server code checks that cited passages genuinely support each claim, screens outputs for PII and off-policy content, and enforces permissions, dollar caps, and business rules (Anthropic, Dec 2024).

Repeatability. Constrain outputs where repeatability matters (structured output schemas, enumerated values, and a pinned thinking level) while keeping sampling parameters at the defaults current reasoning models are tuned for (Gemini 3 guide, 2026). No decoding setting makes a hosted model repeatable: even greedy decoding on shared inference servers varies with the batch a request lands in (in one study, 1,000 identical requests to an open-weight model produced 80 distinct outputs; He et al., Sep 2025). Promise measured consistency (pass^k), and put calculations, thresholds, and final decisions in code.

Answers 6 Reliability, 7 Accountability

D · Route by risk

Low-risk cases run automatically; uncertain or high-impact ones go to a named reviewer.

Customer gets: named reviewer sign-off; the routing threshold and its measured precision
On Google Cloud: the customer's review queue (Pub/Sub or Workflows + Cloud Run), with sign-offs stored in Cloud SQL or Firestore

Route by error cost. Automate low-risk tasks, and route uncertain or high-impact cases, with dry-run previews, to named human reviewers with clear sign-off ownership (RACI: responsible, accountable, consulted, informed).

Calibrate the signal. A model's self-reported confidence ("I am 95% sure") tends to be overconfident (Xiong et al., ICLR 2024), so route on evidence the system can check: whether every cited quote was found in the source, whether every rule criterion resolved, agreement across k independent samples (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), and token log-probabilities where the API exposes them. Tune the routing threshold on the golden set to hit a target precision for auto-approved cases (for example, at least 99% agreement with labels from subject-matter experts (SMEs)), and send everything below it to review.

Prevent rubber-stamping. Show reviewers the evidence before the model's recommendation, track each reviewer's override rate, and occasionally insert known-bad cases to confirm reviews are real (Episode 01 lists approval fatigue as a residual risk).

Answers 7 Accountability

E · Log every step

Every input, source, model version, check, and sign-off is recorded, so any decision can be replayed.

Customer gets: a trace ID that replays any decision
On Google Cloud: Cloud Logging, Cloud Trace, Data Access audit logs (DATA_READ, DATA_WRITE) in a BigQuery sink configured for 400-day+ retention joined on one trace ID, and Security Command Center (SCC) (full wiring in Episode 01)

What to log. Every interaction: input, retrieved chunks, prompt version, pinned model version, thought summaries as supporting evidence, output, and reviewer feedback; redact or tokenize PII before it is written to the log (Episode 01); record step-by-step traces across every tool call so any decision can be reconstructed months later, and expose live drift and quality dashboards to operators.

Provider errors. Treat provider errors as part of observability: isolate HTTP 429 and timeout faults into retry queues with backoff, and record each retry in the trace so a reconstructed run shows which calls failed and why.

Answers 2 Predictability, 4 Model Weights (pinning & inspection), 8 Version Stability, 9 Quality

F · Evaluate and pin

A golden set built with domain experts gates every change, and the model version moves only when it passes.

Customer gets: golden-set results, pass^k consistency (a case passes only if all k runs succeed; Yao et al., ICLR 2025), and failure slices; a pinned model ID and regression report before each cutover
On Google Cloud: ADK Eval or the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform evaluation service; pinned model version IDs; evaluation pipeline in CI

Golden set. Co-create a golden set of real historical cases with the customer's domain specialists, sizing it to the workflow's risk and edge-case coverage; track accuracy, groundedness, hallucination rate, refusal rate, latency, and cost; rerun the set like a regression suite on every prompt, retrieval, or model change; and share the failure breakdown openly. Episode 02 reports pass@k ("at least one win in k tries": run a task k times and count it solved if any attempt passes—useful for coding with unit-test retries), whereas pass^k suits customer-facing consistency claims. Where an LLM judge scores a metric, first measure the judge's agreement with SME labels on a held-out slice; the judge scores offline evaluation only, while request-time verdicts come from code (C · Check outputs in code), as in Episode 02's Verifier.

Pin versions. Upgrade only when the customer's golden set passes. Pinning is temporary: providers retire API model versions on a published schedule, so maintain a migration calendar and run each successor through the customer's golden set before cutover. Self-hosted open weights remove forced retirement altogether.

Training caution. If you fine-tune or run reinforcement learning, keep the reasoning trace out of the reward signal so it stays useful for monitoring (Baker et al., Mar 2025).

Answers 6 Reliability

G · Roll out gradually

Shadow mode first, automation only when the numbers clear the bar, with a kill switch.

Customer gets: shadow-mode comparison results; demotion rules and a kill switch
On Google Cloud: shadow-mode traffic logged to BigQuery for comparison against human decisions, plus Agent Platform Online Monitoring for live production scoring; Cloud Run traffic splitting with Firebase Remote Config or Firestore / Cloud SQL flags for an instant kill switch

Start on a low-risk, high-visibility wedge and earn promotion across stages: Scoped Pilot → Shadow Mode (running silently beside humans to compare outcomes) → Assisted Copilot → Partial Scoped Automation, with automatic demotion when reviewer overrides spike (Episode 02 gives example gates). Every stage is backed by an instant operational kill switch (Hodgson, Oct 2017) that turns off the whole rollout; Episode 02 builds a per-target kill switch inside an agent's control plane, and Episode 01 revokes the agent's identity when it must be cut off from data.

3. Keep the Decision Out of the Model

Agents can plan, write code, and call tools when deterministic checks verify each step; Episode 02 builds that with its autonomy test. Regulated decisions raise the bar: every denial needs a specific, checkable reason, and in some settings software is barred from issuing denials at all. For those, separate reading from deciding (Lyu et al., Jan 2023): the model turns messy text into quoted facts, and code applies the rule.

Worked Case: Prior Authorization for a Lumbar MRI

The case. In California (SB 1120), in Medicare Advantage, and in Medicare's WISeR pilot, software may approve a clear prior-authorization request, while only a licensed clinician may deny one for medical necessity. Take a lumbar MRI policy that covers the scan if either (a) at least six weeks of conservative treatment has failed, or (b) a new or worsening neurological deficit is documented. If the model simply recommended DENY with the note "only 3 weeks of ibuprofen," a busy clinician would likely sign, and nobody would have checked (b).

The model reads. Clinical notes rarely match policy wording: they use abbreviations ("EHL 3/5" for toe-extension weakness), synonyms ("foot drop"), and negations ("denies saddle anesthesia"). Turning that into fixed fields is the model's job. A toolless, single-call Quarantined LLM (Episode 02) returns each fact with a verbatim quote and its page:

Normalized Chart Facts with Verbatim Quotes (Model Output)
{
  "conservative_care": {
    "weeks": 3,
    "date_ranges": ["2026-10-01 to 2026-10-22"],
    "page": 3,
    "quote": "Ibuprofen 400 mg TID x 3 weeks, minimal relief"
  },
  "neuro_deficit": {"documented": null, "page": null, "quote": null}
}

Code decides. Code checks that every quote exists in the chart, does the date math, and applies the policy rule, producing one of three outcomes (Figure 4):

  • Approve: a criterion is met on verified evidence.
  • Fetch: evidence is missing—here (a) is 3 of 6 weeks and (b) is unknown, so a read-only record lookup pulls recent exam notes from the health record and runs them through the same extraction once.
  • Route: anything still unmet goes to a licensed clinician with the verified facts side by side and no recommendation.

In production, the rule is a pinned declarative policy (CEL or HL7 CQL) signed off by clinicians, keeping policy updates out of application code.

4. Summary: Putting It Together

A model's reasons stay hidden behind weights and discarded activations, whether it runs behind an API or on open weights, so trust comes from the system around it. Start from the customer's concern (Table 1), and wrap the model in seven layers: five checks on every request (A–E) and two controls on every change (F–G). Regulation and error cost decide which come first. For high-stakes decisions, keep the decision out of the model: in the prior-authorization case (Section 3), a highly regulated clinical workflow under SB 1120 and CMS-0057-F, the model extracts quoted facts, code evaluates the policy rule, and a licensed clinician owns every denial. Likely Follow-Up Questions

  • "Can't we just make the model deterministic?" Yes, through structure and code; decoding settings alone leave run-to-run variation. Current reasoning models such as Gemini 3 are tuned to run at the default sampling temperature, and Google advises against lowering it; even greedy decoding on shared servers varies with batching. We get predictability from schemas and enumerated outputs, pinned versions and thinking levels, measured pass^k on the customer's golden set, and code for every step that must give the same answer every time.
  • "Why not show the model's chain of thought to the auditor?" Reasoning traces can omit what drove the answer; in hint-based tests, reasoning models often disclosed the hint in fewer than 20% of cases. We log reasoning traces for debugging and give auditors verified quotes, the rules that ran, and a replayable run log.
  • "Doesn't an open-weight model solve the black-box problem?" It solves access, while leaving per-decision understanding unsolved. Open weights let the customer pin, inspect, and self-host the exact checkpoint, but explaining an individual decision from those weights remains research-grade; explanations come from the system layers.
  • "How do you decide what gets auto-approved?" On checkable signals (every quote found, every criterion resolved, agreement across samples) rather than the model's self-reported confidence. The threshold is tuned on the golden set to a target precision, and everything below it goes to a named reviewer.
  • "What happens when the provider retires our pinned model?" Pinning buys time: we keep a migration calendar, run each successor through the customer's golden set, and cut over only when it passes. Self-hosted open weights remove forced retirement altogether.
  • "Isn't an LLM judge in your evaluation just another black box?" It is, until it is calibrated. We measure the judge's agreement with SME labels on a held-out slice, publish that agreement rate, and use the judge only for metrics where it meets the bar.

Common mistakes to avoid:

  • Presenting the reasoning trace as the explanation for a decision.
  • Routing on the model's self-reported confidence.
  • Using an LLM judge without measuring its agreement with experts.
  • Pinning a model version without a migration plan.
  • Building the golden set from synthetic cases alone instead of real historical ones.
  • Treating human review as a safeguard without measuring whether reviewers ever disagree with the model.

The model stays opaque; make everything around it checkable, and keep decisions that matter in code and human hands.
